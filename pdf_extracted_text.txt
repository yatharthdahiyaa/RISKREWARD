Executive summary 
Students decide what to learn from skill lists and headline demand figures. Those show what 
employers list, not what the market pays once you compare like-for-like jobs, and not what an 
employer rewards after hiring. We linked all four supplied datasets to measure those three 
things for five skill areas — coding, maths & statistics, AI/ML, big data, and dashboards & 
storytelling — and turned the results into a Skill Investment Card that recommends a next skill 
with an interval, or says "not enough evidence". 
Every step was verified before the next began: assertions on the data, known-answer tests on 
the statistical methods, null tests, and robustness checks. The main findings: 
1. AI/ML is the most hyped skill but carries no salary premium at entry level. Among 
postings needing 0–3 years, AI/ML has an odds ratio of 0.99 [0.74, 1.33] for a higher 
salary band once experience, city and role family are controlled. Its large raw premium 
mostly reflects that ML jobs sit in better-paid role families. 
2. Coding pays, everywhere. Coding is worth about +12.5% in salary [+8.1%, +17.0%] 
within the same role family, survives all three skill-mapping versions, and is strongest at 
entry level (odds ratio 1.89). 
3. Maths & statistics is what one company rewards most. For junior data scientists, it is 
the strongest predictor of a high salary hike (odds ratio 4.6 per standard deviation, BCa 
interval [2.0, 9.9]; ranked first in 65% of 2,000 resamples), yet the market premium for 
it is modest. This is the clearest "rewarded more than paid" gap. 
4. Dashboards & storytelling are rewarded inside, not paid extra outside. The apparent 
market penalty (odds ratio 0.65) is an artefact of Excel/MIS reporting jobs: with core 
visualisation tools only (Tableau, Power BI, Qlik), the penalty disappears, and at entry 
level those tools carry a premium (1.61). 
5. Your weakest skill says more than your strongest. Among juniors, the weakest of five 
scores predicts a high hike far better than the strongest (AUC 0.85 vs 0.59; difference 
0.26 [0.18, 0.35]). Spread itself does not matter once the overall level is known — the 
lesson is "no weak area", not "be a generalist". 
6. The senior success label can be reproduced almost exactly from the trait scores (one 
cut-off on the total score is 92.5% accurate; a penalised curved model reaches cross-
validated AUC 0.998). We report this as a finding about how the label was built, not as a 
strong model of real-world success. 
The Skill Investment Card passes six tests (including zero monotonicity violations in 1,000 
random profiles) and abstains for profiles outside the data. For example, a final-year student 
strong in coding but weak in statistics and storytelling is advised to invest in maths & statistics 
first (best option in 89% of uncertainty draws).

1. Problem definition and analytics objective 
1.1 Context 
Demand for analytics skills in India is real but uneven across career stages. Naukri's JobSpeak 
index for May 2026 reported AI/ML hiring up 22% year on year against 7% for fresher hiring, 
with fresher growth driven mainly by customer-facing roles [1]. Headlines like these, together 
with recruiter and edtech skill lists, are what students use to choose what to learn. They describe 
demand — how often a skill is asked for — but not price (what it adds to pay in a comparable 
job) or reward (what an employer actually pays more for after hiring). 
SAS supplied four 2024–25 sample datasets that, for the first time in one place, cover all three: 
job postings with skills and salary bands (Analytics Jobs), company-level salary summaries by 
title (Data Science Jobs), measured skills and salary-hike outcomes for junior data scientists 
(JDS), and personality traits and success outcomes for senior data scientists (SDS). 
1.2 Who decides what 
Primary user: a final-year student or early-career analyst (0–3 years) deciding where the next 
six months of learning go — deeper modelling, maths and statistics foundations, data 
engineering, or communication. Secondary readers: universities designing curricula and 
employers writing job descriptions. Hiring managers screening candidates are explicitly not a 
target user (see scope). 
1.3 Core question and objectives 
Core question: Which skills does the market pay for, which does a company reward, 
where do the two disagree — and how sure are we?

1.5 Scope 
In scope: the four supplied files; association-level findings; Indian postings. Out of scope: 
causal claims ("learning X causes a raise"), any tool for screening candidates by skill or 
personality, salary forecasts beyond the data period, and any external data in the analysis (the 
Naukri figure above is context only, consistent with the no-data-transfer rule). 
1.6 What success looks like 
• Skill premiums that survive role-family control, shown as a waterfall: raw → experience 
and city → role family. 
• A Listed vs Paid vs Rewarded comparison with intervals and a verdict that survives 
alternative skill mappings. 
• A senior result reported with a permutation test and a label-construction check, not just 
an AUC. 
• A scorecard whose recommendations carry intervals and abstain outside the data's 
support. 
• Every data fix logged with its row count and its measured effect on results.

2. Approach 
2.1 Overall flow 
Figure 1 shows the pipeline. Three questions — what is listed, what is paid, what is rewarded  
are answered from different files, then joined through a skill-area crosswalk and turned into a 
decision aid. The senior file is used as a career-stage contrast. 
 
Figure 1. Analysis flow. Blue = source data, grey = preparation, orange = the three measurements, green = 
synthesis, black = the student-facing output. 
2.2 Design principles 
1. Compare like with like. A skill can look valuable only because it appears in senior or 
specialist jobs. Every market premium is therefore reported after controlling for 
experience, city and role family. 
2. Show uncertainty everywhere. The junior and senior files are small (139 and 161 rows). 
Every estimate carries an interval; performance is cross-validated and repeated; small-
sample intervals use the bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap. 
3. Verify each step before using it. No step feeds the next until its checks pass (Section 
2.4). 
4. Abstain rather than guess. The decision aid says "not enough evidence" when the 
student's profile is outside the data or two options are statistically tied.

2.5 Tools and reproducibility 
Cleaning and modelling are in Python (pandas, statsmodels 0.15, scikit-learn) with a fixed 
random seed (2026) and raw-file hashes recorded at the start of every run. Every chart is saved 
with the table it plots, and every number in this report is read from those output files. The 
cleaned files are exported as flat CSVs for SAS Viya for Learners, where the reporting layer 
(SAS Visual Analytics) and the model comparison (SAS Model Studio pipelines: logistic 
regression, decision tree, forest, gradient boosting on the same folds) are built; the steps are 
listed in Appendix E.

3.3 Analytics Jobs: salary, experience and location 
Experience strings ("6-10 yrs") parse cleanly for all 14,840 rows (minimum ≤ maximum in 
every row). Salary bands rise steadily with experience (Figure 2): 56% of postings asking for 
0–1 years sit in the lowest band, while 33% of 9+ year postings sit in the top band. This 
monotone pattern is the main sanity check that the salary and experience fields mean what the 
dictionary says. 
 
Figure 2. Salary band by minimum experience (all de-duplicated postings). Rows sum to 100%. 
Locations hold 928 distinct raw tokens, mostly spelling variants and area suffixes such as 
"Bengaluru(Whitefield)". After normalisation, the seven largest cities cover most data-role 
postings (Bengaluru 861, Mumbai 408, Gurugram 368, Delhi NCR 303, Pune 221, Hyderabad 
168, Chennai); the rest are grouped as "Other" in the models. 
Truncation is the most important limitation of this file. 87% of skill lists end in "...", so we 
observe only the first few skills a recruiter typed. A skill being absent therefore does not mean 
the job does not need it. We treat each skill flag as "named among the first listed skills" and re-
check every market result on the 500 data-role postings with complete lists. 
3.4 Separating data roles: a validated filter 
Analytics Jobs mixes data roles with software, finance, marketing and operations jobs. Every 
downstream number depends on which postings count as data roles, so the filter was built and 
tested like a model. We drew random samples, labelled each posting by reading its title and 
skills (rule: is the core work analysing data, statistical or ML modelling, data engineering, or 
BI/reporting?), and scored the filter against those labels. Targets were set in advance: precision 
≥ 0.90 and recall ≥ 0.80.

3.5 Data Science Jobs: the title ladder 
Data Science Jobs summarises 93,005 postings across 642 companies and ten titles. It is 
concentrated: the ten largest employers account for 35% of postings. Weighted by postings, 
median average salary climbs from ₹4.9L for Data Analysts to ₹20.2L for Senior Data 
Scientists and ₹20.5L for Data Architects (Figure 5). Within each title family the "Senior" 
version pays 57–72% more, and a weighted regression with title controls gives +16.5% salary 
per year of minimum experience [14.7%, 18.4%]. Removing the 15 flagged rows changes 
this by half a point (16.0%). 
 
Figure 5. Median average salary by title, weighted by number of postings, with median minimum experience. 
For our student user, this file also identifies fresher-friendly employers: companies posting 
many roles with minimum experience of 0–1 years. The largest include Accenture, Genpact 
and Cognizant (Business Analyst), and TCS, Cognizant and Accenture (Data Analyst); the full 
list (Table 26, Appendix G) feeds the Skill Investment Card. 
3.6 What employers list 
After normalising synonyms (e.g. "ms sql", "mysql", "sql server" → "sql"; "advanced excel" 
→ "excel"), 64 skills appear in at least 50 data-role postings — our minimum support for 
estimating a premium. SQL (21%), Python, SAS, R, data analysis and machine learning (each 
about 18%) lead the list (Figure 6). This is the "Listed" axis: what a student sees in job ads.

Skills are also listed in bundles (Table 27, Appendix G). Machine learning appears with Python

3.8 Junior and senior files 
Both files load cleanly: all JDS scores lie within 1–5 at one decimal; all SDS scores are integers 
between 17 and 68; both outcomes are balanced (52.5% and 52.8% "high"). The largest 
correlation between predictors is 0.45 in each file, so collinearity is not a concern (all variance 
inflation factors < 1.5). Two features need care. JDS shows a strong ceiling effect: between 
18% (big data) and 58% (dashboard & storytelling) of juniors score exactly 5.0, which 
compresses differences at the top. And in both files some IDs are reused by different people; 
keeping all rows changes the cross-validated AUC by less than 0.012. 
Figure 7 shows the junior distributions by outcome. In every area except big data, the "5" 
column is dominated by high-hike juniors — 84% of high-hike juniors score 5 in dashboard & 
storytelling against 30% of low-hike juniors — so the ceiling is itself informative, but it limits 
how finely the top can be ranked. Figure 8 shows the senior traits: high-success seniors score 
markedly higher on extraversion, openness and conscientiousness, while neuroticism barely 
differs.

4. Data analysis 
4.1 Market price: what the market pays 
4.1.1  Model 
For the 3,126 Indian data-role postings, we model the salary band (six ordered levels) with a 
proportional-odds logistic regression. Each of the five skill-area flags enters together, so each 
premium is net of the others. Controls are added in three stages: M0 raw; M1 + minimum 
experience (with a squared term) and city; M2 + role family. An odds ratio (OR) above 1 means 
postings listing the skill are more likely to sit in a higher salary band.

and city (OR 1.68) but most of it disappears once role family is added (1.18, interval crossing 
1): AI/ML pays because ML jobs are better-paid jobs, not because the skill adds pay within a 
comparable job. Dashboards & storytelling looks penalised (OR 0.65, about −11.6% salary) 
— Section 4.3 shows this is driven by how the area is defined. 
4.1.3  Individual skills 
Fitting M2 to each of the 64 skills with at least 50 postings and controlling the false-discovery 
rate leaves 13 skills with a reliable premium or penalty (Figure 10; full table in Appendix D). 
The strongest premiums are for statistical depth — statistical analysis (OR 2.42), logistic 
regression (2.11), model development (2.01) — and for algorithms and programming 
(algorithms 2.20, C++ 1.89, Java 1.60, R 1.47, SAS 1.37, Python 1.36). Tableau carries a 
premium (1.77), while Excel (0.60) and MIS (0.15) carry penalties. Several heavily listed skills 
— SQL, machine learning, Hadoop, big data, deep learning — do not survive the correction, 
which supports H1: being listed often and being paid more are different things.

4.2.1  Generalist or specialist? 
A common student dilemma is whether to be good at everything or excellent at one thing. The 
weakest of a junior's five scores predicts a high hike far better than the strongest (Figure 13): 
AUC 0.85 vs 0.59, a paired-bootstrap difference of 0.26 [0.18, 0.35]. But the average of the 
five predicts best of all (0.92), and once the average is in the model, the spread of scores adds 
nothing (OR 0.76 per SD of spread, [0.38, 1.54]). The honest reading is "don't leave an area 
weak", not "be a generalist": rewards track the overall level, and a single weak area drags it 
down.

returns at the top. H4 is supported, with the caveat below.

4.4.2  Is the label reproducible from the scores? 
Several signs point to a success label constructed from the trait scores themselves. A single 
cut-off on the plain total of all five scores classifies 92.5% of seniors correctly (90.9% when 
the cut-off is chosen inside cross-validation), and the total alone matches the full model's AUC 
(0.961 vs 0.963). Neuroticism, described as a negative trait, has no relationship with success 
on its own (AUC 0.53) yet a positive weight inside the model (OR 3.9) — exactly what happens 
if the label was built from an unweighted total. Finally, success rises in near-steps with the 
number of traits above the median (Figure 15): from 6% with none to 97% with four and 100% 
with five.

4.5 The Skill Investment Card 
4.5.1  Algorithm 
Input: the student's self-rating (1–5) in each area, target role family and experience band. For 
each area: the gap is the distance from the student's rating to the typical high-hike junior 
(median: 5.0 in four areas, 3.8 in big data). The value of closing that gap combines (a) the 
internal reward per rating point from 2,000 bootstrap fits of the junior model and (b) the market 
premium for that area at the student's career stage, drawn from all three crosswalks so the 
mapping uncertainty enters the interval. The two halves are scaled to a common range and 
weighted equally. Output: areas ranked by median value with 80% intervals and the share of 
draws in which each area is the best option. 
Decision rules. Recommend when one area is best in at least 60% of draws; Tie when it is not; 
No gap when the student is within 0.3 points of the high-hike profile everywhere; Abstain when 
any rating is below the lowest observed score (2.2) or the profile is unlike any junior in the data 
(Mahalanobis distance above the 99th percentile).

5.3 Conclusions 
1. Demand is not value. The most listed skills are not the best paid within comparable jobs. 
Students reading skill lists are seeing demand, not price. 
2. For a fresher, AI/ML is a door, not a raise. It moves you into better-paid role families, 
but within those families entry-level AI/ML adds no measurable premium. Coding, data 
engineering and statistics do. 
3. Foundations are undervalued by the market but rewarded after hiring. Maths & 
statistics is the strongest internal reward driver yet only modestly priced in postings — 
the clearest case where a student's investment pays after hiring more than the job ad 
suggests. 
4. "Dashboards" means two different things. Real visualisation tools pay; Excel and MIS 
reporting jobs do not. Storytelling is rewarded internally either way. 
5. Close weak areas before deepening strong ones. Internal rewards follow the overall 
level of skills, so one weak area costs more than one extra-strong area earns. 
6. Check labels before trusting models. The senior label is reproducible from its inputs; 
the high AUC is a property of the data, not evidence of a predictive tool. 
6. Implications 
6.1 For students and early-career analysts 
• Use the Skill Investment Card, not the skill-list headline. It shows the gap, the value 
of closing it, and how sure the estimate is. 
• Do not skip statistics for a shortcut into AI/ML. Statistical depth (regression, model 
development, statistical analysis) carries the largest skill premiums and the strongest 
internal reward. 
• Learn a real visualisation tool, not just Excel. Tableau/Power BI-type skills pay at 
entry level; Excel/MIS reporting roles sit in lower bands. 
• Coding is the safest single investment for a fresher — the largest and most robust 
entry-level premium (OR 1.89). 
• Fix the weakest area first. A balanced profile without a weak spot is what the employer 
in our data rewarded.

35% of postings; several hire at 0–1 years in large numbers.

6.2 For universities 
• Keep, and make visible, a strong statistics and modelling core; the evidence suggests it is 
undervalued by students reading job ads. 
• Teach visualisation with industry tools and pair it with storytelling practice, rather than 
spreadsheet reporting alone. 
• Publish skill guidance with uncertainty and career stage; "AI/ML is in demand" is true 
but incomplete advice for a fresher. 
6.3 For employers and recruiters 
• Job descriptions that list AI/ML for entry-level roles without paying for it attract 
mismatched expectations; listing statistics and storytelling where they are rewarded 
internally would signal more accurately. 
• Internal reward systems that track overall skill level (as here) favour balanced profiles; 
communicating that to juniors would focus their development. 
• Labels used for "success" should be checked for construction from their own inputs 
before being used in any analysis. 
6.4 Limitations 
• Association, not causation. Premiums are what postings with a skill offer in comparable 
jobs, not the raise a person would get from learning it. 
• Truncated skill lists (87%) mean skill flags capture only the first-named skills; the 
complete-list subset agrees in direction but is small (n = 500). 
• One company each for the junior and senior files; their findings are a case study, not 
industry-wide. 
• Self-ratings vs measured scores. The card asks students to rate themselves on a scale 
the company measured by tests; ratings may be optimistic. 
• Single-reviewer labelling. The data-role and role-family gold samples were labelled by 
one reviewer; an independent second labelling and agreement statistic would strengthen 
the filter validation. 
• Crosswalk judgement. Three versions reduce, but do not remove, the subjectivity of 
mapping tools to areas.

B. Known-answer and null tests 
All tests use seed 2026. Ordinal model: latent log-salary = log 4 + 0.12 × experience + 0.25 
× skill + logistic noise (scale 0.35), banded at 3, 6, 10, 15 and 25 LPA; 200 replications of n = 
2,700. Bootstrap: real JDS predictors, standardised, with planted coefficients (0, 0.8, 0.6, 0.3, 
0.9); 60 replications × 400 resamples. Permutation: 200 null datasets of n = 150; one dataset 
with a logistic effect of 1.0. BH: 40 uniform and 10 near-zero p-values, 300 replications. 
Curvature LRT: n = 161, five standard-normal traits, quadratic term 0 or −0.8 on one trait; 
200 replications each. Market null test: the AI/ML flag shuffled 200 times within the real 
data. 
C. Data-role labelling protocol 
Label "data role" if the core work of the job is analysing data, building statistical or machine-
learning models, data engineering (ETL, data warehousing, big-data platforms) or BI/reporting 
development. Label "not a data role" for software development, testing, finance, 
marketing/SEO, sales, operations, administration of infrastructure (including database and
